## Part 1: Alumni

### Cross-cutting evidence (applies to B1-B4)
- [E] Alumni interest in supporting students is common but mostly passive: in a 2022 US survey, 57% wanted to support current or prospective students; of those 44% would mentor and 35% would talk about their careers or help recruitment; interest was 3 in 4 among graduates of the last 10 years (S10, vendor survey, US, so transfer to NL is [I]).
- [E] CASE measures engagement in four modes (philanthropic, volunteer, experiential, communications) across about 400 institutions in 2025 (S11). [I] Mentoring and tutoring sit in the volunteer/experiential modes; CASE's own splits by alumni profession are not available to me (UNVERIFIED: key-findings PDF not machine-readable).
- [E] Mentoring effect sizes are small: attitudes r about .16-.19, career outcomes about .05-.11 (S7). [I] A pitch to alumni that promises large career impact for the student overstates the evidence; promise a bounded, cheap contribution.
- [E] Mentors report higher job satisfaction and organisational commitment than non-mentors (S8); mentor motives split into self-, protégé-, relationship- and organisation-focused (S9). [I] Give mentors a self-focused reward (visible status) next to the helping motive.
- [E] Cold outreach to senior people is low-yield: about 7% reply for C-level, 7.5% open InMail (S16, vendor data, conf low-med).
- [E] Shares of Maastricht alumni: about 3/4 at master's level or higher, about 4/5 in a field-matching sector (S13). [I] The pool is mostly in-field professionals, which supports segmenting by profession.
- Role ladders and cities below are [I] unless a source is named: no Dutch cohort study by profession and years-since-graduation was found (UNVERIFIED, see Gaps G1).

### B1 Finance (banking, asset management, audit, fintech)
1. **Who (5/10/20 yrs)** [I, UNVERIFIED as a cohort fact]. 5y: analyst-to-associate, junior auditor/senior associate, risk or fintech product roles. 10y: vice president, senior manager (audit), portfolio manager, fintech team lead. 20y: director or managing director, partner (audit), CFO/CRO-line roles. Hubs [I]: Amsterdam (Zuidas), Utrecht, Rotterdam, Frankfurt, London, Luxembourg, Zurich, Brussels; Maastricht-region alumni also in Limburg and Aachen/Cologne area (UNVERIFIED, no source). Evidence anchor: only that most UM alumni work in a field-matching sector (S13).
2. **Wants (ranked) / refuses** [I]. Wants: (1) vetted deal-flow of talent (interns, juniors) at low screening cost; (2) peer network across firms and cities; (3) status that is visible internally (e.g. "mentor of the year"); (4) recognition for giving back. Refuses: open DM inbox, sales pitches from fintech peers, anything that looks like broadcasting views on markets. [O] No survey on finance alumni specifically was found.
3. **Inbox protection / yes-triggers** [E+I]. Reply to cold C-level outreach about 7% (S16). [I] Yes-triggers: 20-30 minute fixed slot, pre-filled agenda, specific question, evidence of preparation (CV link, tutor-group record, prior points). [O] time-box figures are practitioner norm, UNVERIFIED as measured preferences.
4. **Incentives** [E/I]. Mentor satisfaction and commitment gains (S8, E); hiring pipeline for graduate programmes [I]; banker's oath culture gives a norm of integrity but no CSR-hours credit evidence (UNVERIFIED: employer CSR-hour rules by firm not retrieved).
5. **Objections/risks**. [E] All Dutch bank staff must take the banker's oath or affirmation (S15) and unlawful disclosure of inside information is prohibited (MAR art. 14, S15). [I] Therefore: no market-sensitive talk in group chat; chat should carry a "no client or deal information" banner; mentoring needs a declared-conflict field (employer, competitor). Time is the largest barrier [I].
6. **Mechanics that fit** [I]. Contact rules (strongest fit), status points as access-ticket, small events (breakfast/lunch, one slot), map by hub city. Open group chat: weak fit because of MAR risk.
7. **Evidence quality**: regulatory facts high (S15); everything about preferences is inference; overall **low-med**.

### B2 Management (general management, operations, entrepreneurs, corporate leadership)
1. **Who** [I, UNVERIFIED]. 5y: management trainee, operations/product manager, junior founder. 10y: head of function, business unit manager, founder/co-founder. 20y: managing director, COO/CEO of SME, board member, serial entrepreneur. Cities [I]: Randstad, Brabant (Eindhoven region), Limburg, Düsseldorf/Cologne, Brussels, London, Berlin, Singapore for internationals.
2. **Wants/refuses** [I]. Wants: access to talent; to meet peers outside their own company; founder-to-founder introductions; recognition. Refuses: administrative requests, event-fatigue, "pick my brain" asks. [E] 44% of interested US alumni would mentor (S10), so willingness exists; format preference not reported by profession.
3. **Inbox protection** [E+I]. About 7% reply to cold C-level outreach (S16). [I] Entrepreneurs are fastest to reply when the ask is a hiring or a customer opportunity; a student-only ask needs a very small box (15-20 min) and a stated outcome.
4. **Incentives** [E/I]. Giving-back and mentor satisfaction (S8, S9); hiring juniors and interns [I]; reputation inside the university network [I]. Employer CSR hours: UNVERIFIED.
5. **Objections** [I]. Time; fear of being asked for a job or investment; business confidentiality (low compared with B1/B3).
6. **Mechanics** [I]. Map, events by city, mentor flag with capacity limit (e.g. max N open mentees), project-based tutoring/challenge with a company case, status points converting to "featured company" visibility.
7. **Evidence quality**: **low** (inference on generic mentoring evidence S7-S10).

### B3 Law (corporate and tax lawyers, in-house counsel, legal advisers)
1. **Who** [I, UNVERIFIED]. 5y: associate / junior in-house. 10y: senior associate, counsel, in-house senior counsel. 20y: partner, general counsel, judge/regulator roles. Cities [I]: Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Brussels, Luxembourg, London, Frankfurt. Note: UM has a law faculty; SBE alumni in law are fewer, UNVERIFIED which faculty supplies this segment for UniCircle.
2. **Wants/refuses** [I]. Wants: peers for referrals across jurisdictions; hiring of clean, prepared junior candidates; visibility. Refuses: any channel that could touch client information; unsolicited requests for legal advice (also a professional-liability issue) [I].
3. **Inbox protection** [I]. High: lawyers are advised on the core value of confidentiality (S14). Yes-trigger: a CV-first, scoped question ("how did you choose tax vs corporate"), 20-minute slot.
4. **Incentives** [E]. Dutch firms publish pro bono volumes: De Brauw logged 8,500+ hours on 75 matters by 211 lawyers in 2023 (S18); the Netherlands has no mandatory pro bono hours and pro bono earns no CPD credit; some firms count pro bono toward billable targets (S18, older survey). [I] Student mentoring could be framed as a firm social-impact activity if the firm accepts it; UNVERIFIED that firms count mentoring.
5. **Objections** [E]. Five core values incl. independence, integrity, confidentiality, set by Advocatenwet art. 10a (S14); the duty of confidentiality is the basis of legal privilege (NOvA, source summary). [I] Conflict of interest is a live issue: a lawyer should not mentor a student who works for an opposing party. Add a conflict-declaration field and "no legal advice" disclaimer.
6. **Mechanics** [I]. Contact rules and mentor opt-in with topic whitelist; events with an explicit no-client-talk rule; group chat only as moderated topic rooms; points as quiet status, not leaderboards.
7. **Evidence quality**: regulatory facts high (S14), pro bono facts med (S18), behaviour **low**.

### B4 Consultancy (strategy and management consulting, Big Four advisory)
1. **Who** [E low + I]. Career blogs describe analyst-to-partner timelines of about 7-12 years at top strategy firms and note high attrition before partner, with a former consultant estimating about 90% leave before partner (S19, career blogs; conf low). 5y: consultant / senior consultant; 10y: manager / senior manager, or industry exit (corporate strategy, startups); 20y: partner, or C-suite in industry. Cities [I]: Amsterdam, Utrecht, Düsseldorf, Frankfurt, London, Zurich, Brussels.
2. **Wants/refuses** [I]. Wants: recruitment pipeline (consultancies recruit heavily at graduates), alumni-of-firm networks, client introductions after exit. Refuses: case-interview requests in bulk; free consulting for students' companies.
3. **Inbox protection** [E+I]. About 7% C-level reply rate (S16). [I] Consultants are used to structured asks: short written brief, 30 minutes, specific deliverable (e.g. review a case answer).
4. **Incentives** [I]. Recruiting credit (referrals), personal brand, giving back; Big Four CSR hours: UNVERIFIED (search returned nothing on Dutch Big Four volunteering hours).
5. **Objections** [I]. Time (billable utilisation), client confidentiality and independence rules for audit-linked advisory, conflict when a client is the student's employer.
6. **Mechanics** [I]. Case clinics / mock-case mentoring slots (tutoring format), event nights by city, points that unlock "approach" with a message template, contact rules with weekly cap.
7. **Evidence quality**: **low** (career blogs plus inference).

## Part 2: Students

### C0 Scientific standing of colour/DISC typologies (state first)
- [E, med] DISC-type tools are widely used but the independent evidence for predictive validity is weak; reported reliability comes largely from vendor reports; critics cite the Barnum effect and argue DISC structure is better explained by Big Five traits (S1; Wikipedia summary of critics, so cite as secondary). A German evaluation reportedly found reliability largely met but validity not met (S1, via secondary summary, UNVERIFIED primary).
- [E] The MBTI, a similar four-type tool, was judged by Pittenger (1993) to fall short of standards for psychological tests (S2). A 2017 systematic review found only 7 of 221 candidate MBTI studies met inclusion criteria (secondary search summary; UNVERIFIED primary, https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/2017-randall.pdf).
- [E, high] The closest education analogue, learning-style matching, lacks evidence: Pashler et al. (2008) found no adequate evidence base for using learning-style assessments in practice, and very few experimental designs could test it (S3).
- [I] A colour label must not be treated as a fact about a person, must not gate access or be shown to others, and must not be used for selection or grading.
- [I] **Legitimate uses for a product team**: (a) design personas / hypotheses that you then test with behaviour data; (b) segment by observable behaviour (first click, request type) and call it a "mode", not a trait; (c) let users self-select a "how I work" preference they can change; (d) offer all options to all users so the label never restricts. [O] Also treat it as a communication aid, not a measurement.
- [I] Counter-risk: AVG/GDPR processing of inferred personality profiles; avoid storing inferred type (UNVERIFIED legal view, not researched).

### Dutch / Maastricht context (for all three)
- [E] UM: more than 50% of students come from abroad (S12). SBE: 6,500+ students and 60%+ international per SBE page vs 4,200+ per Times Higher Education; the brief's 4,200 matches the THE figure and conflicts with SBE's own page (S12; discrepancy flagged).
- [E] SBE tutorial groups have 8-15 students plus a tutor, who guides the process (S12). [I] Group identity is strong: "who was in your tutorial group" is a built-in hook; also mixed-nationality groups can lower fit for some students (UM inclusivity research, https://maastrichtuniversity.nl/node/41085, abstract only).
- [E] Help seeking: avoidant and "executive" (get the answer) help-seeking correlate negatively with achievement, instrumental help-seeking positively (small effects), and students prefer informal friends over formal tutors (S17).

### C-red (action-oriented)
1. **Observable behaviour** [I, hypotheses to test]. First click: "find a mentor / internship / who works at X" or a direct message button; asks short, outcome-first ("intro to hiring manager"); skips profile completion; short sessions; high repeat if a result arrives within a day.
2. **Needs**. Tutoring: quick answers (risk of "executive help-seeking", S17: gets the answer, learns less; design the tutor to ask one reversible question). Mentoring: one concrete ask, answered within 48h. Peer groups: project groups with a deadline. Internships/events: pitch nights, case competitions.
3. **Points/rankings** [E+I]. Meta-analysis: gamification effects on learning are small and stable only for cognitive outcomes; motivational and behavioural effects less stable; competition combined with collaboration worked best for behaviour (S4). Quasi-experiment: leaderboards plus mandatory badges lowered intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores in one course (S5, N about 71, med). [I] Red users probably respond to visible rank, but ranking can crowd out autonomy (S6). Risk of harm: gaming points via low-quality tutoring (quantity over quality).
4. **Adaptation** [I]. Onboarding: "What do you want to do in the next 4 weeks?" with 3 outcome buttons; tone: short, no explanations; show progress toward "right to approach" as a task list; team leaderboards (collaborative) not individual.
5. **Evidence quality**: behaviour descriptions are inference (no validated link between DISC red and platform behaviour was found, UNVERIFIED); gamification evidence S4-S6 is real but not type-specific.

### C-blue (thinker)
1. **Observable behaviour** [I]. First click: read how points and contact rules work, check mentor profiles' depth, filters; asks precise questions; long sessions with low message volume; drafts messages and abandons; reads FAQ before first request.
2. **Needs**. Tutoring: explanations with sources, time to reflect; mentoring: a mentor with matching field and a written agenda; peer groups: small, structured; events: seated, with a topic and Q&A.
3. **Points/rankings** [E+I]. Meta-analysis suggests narrative-based gamification beat points/badges/leaderboards in motivation (a 2026 meta-analysis cited by search summary, UNVERIFIED primary, https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/download/5188/1617/28002); SDT predicts that controlling rewards weaken autonomy (S6). [I] Public ranking is most likely to raise anxiety here; show private progress, not rank. Risk: perfectionism blocks first contact; the tool must make the first ask small.
4. **Adaptation** [I]. Onboarding: show how contact rules protect alumni (builds trust); provide message templates and a "preview what the mentor sees"; tone: precise, with the reason for each field; no countdown timers.
5. **Evidence quality**: inference; help-seeking meta-analysis shows adaptive (instrumental) help-seeking helps (S17).

### C-social (connector)
1. **Observable behaviour** [I]. First click: tutorial-group or city group, the people tab, chat; asks "who else is going / who from my group"; high message volume; low conversion to formal requests unless introduced; shares the event with friends.
2. **Needs**. Tutoring: group tutoring and peer-buddy; mentoring: group mentoring, alumni "office hours" with 5-8 students; peer groups: tutorial-group channels; internships: referrals via friends; events: social, repeat.
3. **Points/rankings** [E+I]. SDT: relatedness is a basic need (S6); S4 says combining competition with collaboration was effective for behavioural outcomes. [I] Social users respond to team/group points and thank-you signals; risk of harm: public ranking excludes low-ranked members and promotes cliques, especially in mixed-nationality groups.
4. **Adaptation** [I]. Onboarding: "Which tutorial group / city / society are you in?"; tone: warm, names of groups; invite-a-friend; group streaks.
5. **Evidence quality**: inference; S17 notes students prefer informal help from friends over formal sources.
